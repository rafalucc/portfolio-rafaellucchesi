design system as infrastructure for AI

CENTRAL THESIS: Design Systems are no longer just collections of reusable artifact libraries for digital products. In the AI era, they are becoming design infrastructure that guides both humans and machines.

September, 2026

By Rafael Lucchesi

summary

A complete Design System combines knowledge, rules, artifacts, product patterns, accessible code, and governance. AI expands this structure, but it does not replace the human work required to make it trustworthy.

Many organizations already use AI to accelerate the creation of digital interfaces. Some go further, turning their Design Systems into infrastructure capable of providing context to generative agents. The result is fewer product inconsistencies, faster design decisions, and teams with greater delivery capacity.

For a decade, we treated Design Systems as collections of reusable components. This approach delivered real gains: greater visual consistency across interfaces, higher productivity, and smoother collaboration between Design and Engineering.

With the arrival of generative models, specialized agents, and the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the Design System takes on a new role. It stops being a set of interface libraries and becomes the knowledge base that explains how the product should be designed. When agents encounter a consistent, robust system, they accelerate delivery; when they encounter an inconsistent one, they simply reproduce that inconsistency faster.

In this article, I share eight essential actions for structuring a Design System where AI agents can help accelerate the construction of infrastructure without compromising quality.

glossary

design system

According to Theresa Fessenden (NN/g, 2021), a Design System is a complete set of standards intended to manage Design at Scale using reusable components and patterns. From this definition, I understand the role of systematizing Design as enabling it to scale within a digital product without losing interface consistency or the quality of the end-user experience.

infrastructure

Infrastructure is the essential foundation that allows a structure to be built and remain standing. In the context of a Design System, it can be understood as the set of standards, libraries, and foundational resources required for an activity, organization, or system to operate and remain sustainable.

generative AI (gen AI)

Generative AI is a category of AI capable of creating content from patterns identified across large volumes of data. According to IBM, Gen AI uses deep-learning models to identify relationships within data. It operates as a probabilistic system: it analyzes the context it receives and calculates which elements are most likely to form a response.

model context protocol (MCP)

An open protocol that standardizes how AI agents connect to external data sources and tools. In practice, it allows an agent to query tokens directly in Figma or code in a repository instead of relying only on what was written in the prompt.

skill

A Skill is a package of instructions, examples, and resources that teaches an AI agent how to perform a specific task according to an organization's way of working. In a Design System, a Skill can describe how to build a form or review tokens, including which sources to consult and the criteria used to validate the result.

what

the second generation of design systems

The first generation of Design Systems solved a problem of visual consistency and agile interface maintenance. The second addresses a product design knowledge problem. The Design System now distributes validated context to both people and generative agents.

The Design System must therefore provide context that faithfully reflects the product's brand identity. This reduces the chances of an agent generating generic layouts that look like those of any other organization.

the new bottleneck will be context quality

When producing a screen becomes easier, value shifts elsewhere: to organizing the design decisions that make the product unique. The bottleneck moves from a lack of artifacts to the taxonomy of elements, and from isolated creation to centralized governance.

This explains why Design System tools alone have not solved inconsistency. Each one does its job well:

  • Figma Variables represents modes and themes.
  • Style Dictionary transforms tokens for multiple platforms.
  • Storybook documents and tests artifacts.
  • Playwright detects changes in recurring flows.
  • GitHub runs repository automations.
  • MCP connects agents to canonical sources.

But none of them decides where brand identity ends and the product begins. That decision belongs to the Design System and to the quality of the context it provides.

the new operational layer of the design system

AI is no longer just a code-generation tool; it has become a new operational layer of the Design System. Rather than treating it as a collection of isolated automations, it is more useful to see it as part of the system's entire cycle: map, formalize, systematize, structure, connect, implement, verify, and learn.

why AI makes this shift urgent

Generative models are highly capable of combining patterns, but the quality of what they produce depends on the context they can access. Imagine rules scattered across Figma files, outdated pages, private conversations, duplicated components, and legacy code. In that scenario, the agent has to fill the gaps with probabilistic solutions. The result may look correct while still violating important product rules.

Scalability does not come from mechanically repeating interfaces. It comes from the ability to reuse good design decisions without erasing differences between products, features, and contexts. This is where the Build for Reuse principle matters: turning recurring solutions into accessible, applicable knowledge.

I don't believe the answer is writing enormous prompts. It is structuring scalable sources. In practice, instead of disconnected decisions, you work with clearly defined layers:

  • Brand tokens, which define style properties;
  • Components, which materialize interactive behaviors;
  • Blocks, which structure recurring solutions;
  • Templates, which organize pages and flows;
  • Patterns, which guide experience decisions.

Documentation connects all of this through rules, parameters, and usage contexts. Together, these layers form a single source of truth capable of guiding both people and agents. The Design System stops being a catalog of parts and becomes the organization's operational memory: a record of how it decides, builds, validates, and learns.

how to build design infrastructure with AI

1. start with recurrence, not the artifact

The first step is to investigate where the organization repeats design decisions. Compare products and features: how does each one create, edit, delete, search, filter, navigate, and respond to feedback? Gather production screens, Figma frames, support data, and code implementations.

→ AI tools can help classify screenshots, extract text, group naming patterns, and locate duplicates. Interpretation, however, still requires the judgment of a Designer or someone from Product and Engineering. The result should not be a list of missing artifacts. It should be a map of inconsistencies prioritized by repeated actions, user impact, maintenance cost, and reuse potential.

2. turn evidence into explicit rules

Once recurrence is identified, formulate the decision that needs to be reused. Suppose different products use different labels, hierarchies, and confirmations for common actions. The question is not “which component looks better?” The question is: “what is the pattern?”

→ The new pattern needs to state when to use it, when not to use it, its variations, behaviors, accessibility requirements, and best practices. These guidelines help teams and are essential for AI agents because agents do not have access to the organization's implicit knowledge—the things “everyone knows” but nobody has written down.

3. build the design system layers in the right order

It is important to build the Design System in the right order because each layer has a role and complements the others:

  • Tokens encode concepts such as color, typography, spacing, and themes;
  • Components turn those decisions into interface contracts;
  • Blocks organize recurring combinations;
  • Templates define recurring page structures.

→ Following this order avoids two common mistakes. The first is creating components with too many variations in an attempt to cover every scenario. The second is publishing rigid templates that only work for one specific product case. The rule is simple: each layer should abstract only what has already demonstrated recurrence.

4. define canonical sources before connecting agents

Instead of explaining how the product should work in every prompt, give the agent structured sources it can consult on its own. This reduces both the effort required to write prompts and the margin for execution errors.

→ You do not need to centralize everything in a single tool. What matters is being clear about which source is responsible for each design decision. One possible setup is:

  • Figma Variables to express brand style decisions as tokens;
  • Figma as the design reference;
  • Storybook to reflect the actual implementation of each artifact;
  • GitHub as the code source;
  • Documentation as the technical reference;
  • Skills to turn internal rules into reusable processes;
  • MCP to connect these sources to agents.

This structure is not a rule. It is a starting point that you can adapt to your organization's reality.

5. document for people and machines

Good documentation for people is clear, objective, scannable, and oriented around everyday tasks. Documentation for AI agents shares those qualities but needs to be even more structured and specific. It should state purpose, inputs, outputs, constraints, examples, exceptions, and validation criteria.

→ Skills can organize this knowledge around recurring tasks such as building a form, reviewing tokens, creating a read page, or migrating a legacy component. Instead of asking AI to simply “follow the Design System,” the organization describes the process, identifies allowed sources, and requires testing evidence.

6. use MCP to provide specific context, not full access

MCP access should expand in stages according to risk. Technical capability is not the same as authorization. Every action needs appropriate permissions and traceable access:

  • Read-only. The agent consults tokens, components, guidelines, and usage examples;
  • Low-risk tasks. The agent generates stories and implementation tests;
  • Structural changes. Changes to frameworks, tokens, patterns, and releases remain subject to human review.

Suggested flow: Organized Figma → Connected Figma MCP → Agent with Design System Skills → Code reusing the library → Storybook and tests → Agent with repository access for a Pull Request → Human review → Release.

7. treat quality as part of generation

Rapidly generated code still needs to prove that it works. The pipeline should verify states, responsiveness, accessibility, and interaction. Storybook provides an environment for building components in isolation, documenting variations, and running tests before any change reaches the product.

→ Design QA completes the automation. Tests detect expected violations. Human review evaluates appropriateness, hierarchy, clarity, and context. Together, they reduce rework and make quality verifiable rather than an abstract promise.

8. close the loop with learning

Design Systems are never finished. They become more reliable as they learn from real usage. That is why federated governance tends to work better. The system's core preserves coherence and infrastructure, while product teams contribute problems, evidence, pilots, and improvements.

→ The flow can include proposals, triage, discovery, RFCs, pilots, testing, and evolution. AI can participate in all of these stages, but canonical decisions must remain visible and have clear owners.

conclusion

Generative AI reduces the time and cost of producing a new screen. That alone does not reduce the cost of maintaining it. An agent can generate six themes, dozens of components, and complete templates in very little time. But without a well-structured source of context, it can also accelerate six different forms of inconsistency.

That is why the starting point is not the tool; it is the organization of decisions. Companies that structure their decisions will be able to use agents to increase quality and autonomy. Those that connect AI to fragmented systems will tend to produce inconsistencies faster and faster.

The future of the Design System is not a larger library. It is infrastructure capable of teaching the organization, and the machines working with it, to make better product design decisions.
